Meet the team

How to play

You run an outpost on the Moon. Your job: keep 3 crew members healthy for 3 shifts to earn stars, and run experiments to win the science star.

Why the Sun matters: solar panels turn sunlight into the power your outpost runs on. But the Sun also fires storms, and the Moon has no air to block them. So the Sun is your power and your danger.

How a mission goes

  1. 1. Hear the Sun reportAt the start, Captain Rae shows you a real photo of the Sun. A ringed bright patch has fired before, so it may fire again. Each shift she tells you the storm chance and how big a storm would likely be. It is a forecast, so it can be wrong.
  2. 2. Place your batteriesBatteries are your power. Your solar panels make 5 each shift. A shadow on the panels takes 1 away, and bright sun adds 1. The four rooms want more than you have, so drag each battery into a room and choose which room goes short.
  3. 3. Watch the shiftIt plays by itself. If the Sun flashes, a storm is on its way. If the shelter is not ready, you get 10 seconds for a last-chance move. It costs more: a rushed shelter needs 1 extra battery, and you can take batteries from one room only. Being ready before the storm is cheaper.

What each room does

  1. Air room: needs 2 batteriesIt keeps the air fresh. With only 1 battery the air gets thin and 1 crew member gets hurt. With none, the whole crew gets hurt.
  2. Farm: power it every shiftThe crew eats 3 meals a shift, 1 each. Each day starts with 6 fresh meals from Earth, so food runs out in shift 3. Lettuce needs 4 growth steps, and each battery gives 1 step a shift. So 2 batteries means a harvest in 2 shifts. A harvest makes 3 meals, enough for one shift. That is exactly what shift 3 needs. Once the lettuce is growing, it needs power every shift in a row. One shift with no power, and it wilts.
  3. Shelter: 1, 2 or 3 batteriesIt blocks storms: 1 for a small one, 2 for medium, 3 for big. Each battery it is short hurts one crew member. When it holds, the lab measures the storm safely, and that counts as experiments.
  4. Lab: 1 experiment per batteryExperiments are why the crew came to the Moon. Run 4 with a healthy crew to win the science star. But the lab does not keep anyone safe.

Your crew

  1. Captain Rae, Mission ControlShe is on Earth, not on the Moon. She sends the Sun report and the order of the day. A radio call to Earth and back takes more than 2 seconds, so in an emergency the crew has to decide alone.
  2. Nova, plant scientistRuns the farm. If she is hurt, the lettuce needs 1 more growth step (5, not 4). If she is sick, 2 more.
  3. Kai, power engineerKeeps the solar panels working. If he is hurt, you get 1 fewer battery. If he is sick, 2 fewer.
  4. Juno, medicHeals the crew. If she is hurt or sick, she can only heal herself. Once she is fine, she heals everyone at once.

♥♥ Fine, ♥♡ Hurt, ♡♡ Sick. Hurt is 1 step down. Sick is 2 steps down, so it is worse. A good shift heals 1 step: hurt needs 1 good shift, and sick needs 2. A good shift means enough air, enough meals, no new harm, and a healthy Juno.

Stars

Stars are for looking after your crew. 1 star: you finish the mission. 2 stars: only one astronaut is hurt at the end. 3 stars: everyone is healthy at the end.

Some days the real Sun is too strong, and no plan can keep everyone healthy. That happens on real missions too. On those days the game compares you with the best possible plan, so you can still earn 3 stars.

Science star

Earn 3 stars and run 4 experiments to win the science star. A blocked storm counts as experiments too: 1 for a small storm, 2 for medium, 3 for big.

Upgrade cards

Win the science star in a daily mission and you get an upgrade card. You choose when to use it, on the battery screen. A card makes one mission easier, then it is gone.

Captain's order of the day

Each day Captain Rae gives one extra order, like "Harvest the lettuce by the end of shift 2". It is a new puzzle every day. Finish it in the daily mission and you win a new building, with a real NASA fact. Then choose where to build it on your Moon land, on the home page. If the Sun makes the order impossible, the game says so and you still get the building.

Space pets

Finish your first daily mission and a space pet joins your Moon land. Play 3 days in a row to meet the next one. They are real species that NASA has studied in space: a water bear, a bobtail squid, a fruit fly and a mouse. Tap one to read its real fact.

Try again

After a mission you can press "Try this Sun again" to replay the same real day with a new plan. Replays are practice, so they change nothing.

Fun facts

    About the data

    • Real: each flare's date, time, size class, sunspot number and place on the Sun, and the clues built from them. Source: NASA DONKI (Space Weather Database Of Notifications, Knowledge, Information) at CCMC.
    • The daily mission uses yesterday's date (UTC). The game first asks NASA for the newest records. If it cannot reach NASA, it uses its saved copy from 14 Nov 2026, and says so. Practice missions pick a saved past day. On 15 Nov 2026 we checked 87 saved records (1 to 5 Feb and 5 to 11 Nov 2026) against NASA, and all 87 matched.
    • Simplified: in the game every flare brings a storm to the outpost. In real life only some flares send harmful particles toward the Moon. A calm day means DONKI lists no flare for that date.
    • Real science, squeezed: a flare's light reaches the Moon in about 8 minutes and the harmful particles come later. The game squeezes that wait into a few seconds.
    • Batteries: the outpost makes 5 a shift, loses 1 when a shadow falls on the solar panels, and gains 1 in bright sun. That is a game rule. It is based on a real fact: near the Moon's south pole the Sun stays low on the horizon, and long shadows can get in the way of solar power. Source: NASA, The Lunar South Pole Region.
    • Game rules, not data: the storm chance and likely size (when a storm is coming, the forecast says "high" 3 times in 4 and names the right size 3 times in 4), the extra battery for a rushed shelter, the battery counts, meals, experiments, crop growth, upgrade cards, and the crew rules. They are small whole numbers so a child can count them: 3 crew eat 1 meal each per shift, and 1 lab battery runs 1 experiment. The true part of the crew rules is that a sick crew cannot do all its work.
    • The Sun picture is a real photo from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO) for the mission date, fetched through the Helioviewer service. It is taken in ultraviolet light, which our eyes cannot see, so NASA colours it red. Bright patches are the busy regions where flares happen. If the photo cannot load, the game shows a drawing instead. The ring that marks a sunspot is placed from the NASA flare record, or estimated from the Sun's rotation on a day the spot did not fire, so it is approximate.
    • Stars: the game scores how well you looked after the crew, compared with the best plan that was possible for that day's real storms. It works this out by trying every way to share the batteries. On a day when no plan can keep everyone healthy, it says so, and you are scored against the best plan.
    • Captain's order of the day and the Moon land: the orders are game goals, picked from the date. The game checked every storm pattern in advance to know when an order is impossible. Each building on the Moon land shows a real fact with a link to its NASA page. The line about a lettuce crop taking 33 days is from NASA's Veggie photo notes.
    • Space pets: the four pets are real species from NASA space biology research. Each one links to its NASA page, and NASA shares data from such studies in its Open Science Data Repository (OSDR). In the game they roam the Moon in little air bubbles. That part is for fun: real animals have only flown inside spacecraft.
    • The science star: a blocked storm counts as experiments. That is a game rule. The real part: NASA's Orion spacecraft carries radiation sensors, and one of them can warn the crew to take shelter. Source: NASA, Artemis I radiation measurements.
    • Talking to Earth: a radio signal takes a little over 1 second to reach the Moon, so a question and its answer take more than 2 seconds. That is why the crew makes the last-chance move alone. Source: NASA, Earth Calling.
    • Shifts are 8 hours each on the crew's 24 hour clock (UTC). Each "Did you know?" fact links to its NASA page on the Fun facts page.
    • Music: the game makes its own background music. There is no music file. The Music button switches it off, and it goes quieter while a character talks.
    • Voices: the Voice button makes the voices talk at 2x. A Skip button moves on by one sentence. The game uses your device's built-in voice to read lines aloud. Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere. The voice sounds different on each device, and you can switch it off with the Sound button.
    • Your outpost is saved in this browser only. There is no account.
    • Daily totals: when you finish the daily mission, the game sends 3 things to a shared counter: the mission date, your stars, and whether your whole crew stayed healthy. It sends no name and nothing about you. The game shows only the totals for the day.
